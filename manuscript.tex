\begin{abstract}
Using an open estate-scale optimization framework, we test whether the climate-optimal level of forest harvesting can be identified from current harvested wood product (HWP) evidence. Ecosystem carbon (CBM-CFS3 carbon yield curves) and HWP carbon (decay plus substitution) enter the objective exactly; we solve 25,000 emission-minimizing harvest schedules across five timber supply areas in interior British Columbia, spanning solid-wood half-lives of 1-200 years and displacement factors of 0-2.4 tC/tC. In every landscape the emission-minimizing harvest level flips from its unit's low-displacement harvest level toward the volume-maximizing level across a displacement-factor band; the flip midpoint at a 100-year half-life ranges 0.95-1.61 tC/tC and overlaps empirically reported substitution estimates. Which landscape is managed shifts the threshold about as much as moving half-life from 1 to 200 years, and one landscape optimally ceases harvesting below about 0.5 tC/tC. The climate-optimal harvest level is therefore not a property of the forest alone: it is set by product lifetime and substitution parameters that current evidence bounds loosely and that negotiated product standards could pin down. We argue the harvest-carbon debate should move from proving that harvesting is good or bad toward specifying the product attributes on which its climate value depends.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
The interactions between  forest management paradigms and carbon dynamics are of increasing importance as the impacts of climate change are being realized \citep{Nabuurs2007Forestry}. Forests ability to remove atmosphere carbon and store carbon in biomass have been recognized as a having large climate change mitigation potential \citep{hurteau2021role}. Since 1990, it has been estimated that global forests have removed approximately one-third of the atmospheric \ac{CO$_2$} produced per year from the combustion of fossil fuels  \citep{pan2011large}. As trees accumulate biomass through photosynthesis, the carbon sequestered is distributed across multiple carbon pools, including above-ground biomass (leaves, branches, stems), below-ground biomass (roots, rhizomes), \ac{DOM}  (deadwood and litter), and soils. Using carbon pools, allows for the examination of differences in the amount of carbon stored in each pool and the rate in which carbon moves between different pools. Quantification of carbon storage, flux and flow in forested ecosystems is the starting point for determining how different forest management and \acp{HWP} change forest carbon dynamics. 

\subsection{forest management and how this impacts forest carbon and HWP}


\acp{HWP} are also an important component in how forests affect the carbon cycle, impacting the flux between stored carbon and the atmosphere. The rate at which carbon stored in \acp{HWP} is returned to the atmosphere depends on their type, usage, and end-of-life management \citep{skog1998carbon}. In 2013, the \ac{IPCC} updated guidelines to mandate the inclusion of the \acp{HWP} carbon pool when estimating total anthropogenic \ac{GHG} emissions and removals in the land-use, land-use change, and forestry sector \citep{hiraishi20142013}. Beyond carbon storage, \acp{HWP} can be a substitute for \ac{GHG}-intensive, non-renewable materials, such as concrete and steel, which are the most widely used construction materials globally \citep{geng2017review}, thereby reducing emissions through material substitution. In addition, wood-based bioenergy can replace fossil fuels and natural gas, providing an energy substitution effect \citep{geng2017review}. These substitutions highlight the potential of \acp{HWP} and the forest sectors role in mitigating climate change.

Quantifying forest resources and forecasting forest dynamics are fundamental to effective forest management planning \citep{gunn2007models}. Forest modeling has been extensively employed to help managers understand forest behavior, anticipate future changes, and make strategic, tactical, and operational decisions across spatial and temporal scales. Developing long-term strategies is essential for optimizing the use of finite forest resources while maintaining their ecosystem services \citep{martin2013optimization}. \Acp{SFMM} provide a framework for planning activities that address diverse objectives. Historically, \acp{SFMM} have focused on ensuring a continuous wood supply, prioritizing economic objectives. However, contemporary management emphasizes a balance between multiple objectives where wood supply, economics and ecosystem values (including carbon storage and sequestration), are managed concurrently \citep{gunn2007models}. 

To assess the impacts of forest management on carbon dynamics, balance timber and carbon objective trade-offs and identify optimal forest and timber management strategies, a robust forest carbon modeling framework is essential. Such a framework should integrate carbon indicators (e.g., total forest carbon stock and net forest carbon emissions) into \acp{SFMM} by linking them directly to existing \acp{FCBM} and \acp{WPCM}. While similar analyses can be run manually by exporting data from a \ac{SFMM} into a \ac{FCBM} and \ac{WPCM} for each interval of interest, that approach is labor-intensive and evaluates carbon only as a post-hoc product of management rather than as a manageable objective. 
In contrast, an integrated modeling framework enables efficient identification of management plans that satisfy multiple objectives and constraints via optimization algorithms. Historically, many \acp{SFMM} prioritized wood supply as the objective function, but over the past two decades, efforts have increasingly focused on maximizing forest carbon stocks to enhance climate mitigation.

\cite{meng2003combining} first incorporated carbon indicators into\acp{SFMM} by applying conversion factors from \ac{FCBM} to estimate biogenic carbon in commercial wood and soil. \cite{bourque2007optimizing} refined this approach by setting forest ecosystem carbon storage as an objective; however, they only considered two general carbon pools (biomass and soil) and used simplified conversion factors, limiting accuracy. Building on this, \cite{hennigar2008novel} and \cite{neilson2008optimal} integrated \ac{CBM-CFS3}, derived carbon yield curves representing detailed carbon pools, including softwood and hardwood biomass, DOM and snags, in \acp{SFMM}, enabling temporal modeling of ecosystem carbon stocks for different  types of stand. Their frameworks optimized ecosystem carbon stocks but did not consider \acp{HWP} or their substitution effects.

No estate-scale optimization study we are aware of carries the full ecosystem and \acp{HWP} carbon ledgers inside the objective \emph{and} sweeps the \ac{HWP} parameters over the range of values reported in the empirical literature; studies close to this either optimize ecosystem carbon only or fix decay and substitution centrally. Recent comparators illustrate the gap: multi-objective value-chain optimization allocates \acp{HWP} across products but holds the harvest level fixed \citep{boiger2025optimizing}; global forest-sector models endogenize prices, leakage and product decay but at scales and conventions that do not map onto a management-unit harvest level \citep{daigneault2022future}; and national syntheses document model-specific \ac{HWP} parameters while noting the missing integration with substitution economics \citep{lucey2024synthesis}. The direct precursor is the TSA 24 framework of \cite{yan2025bcforest}, which generated and validated the open \ac{ws3} + libCBM carbon-yield-curve workflow and first swept half-life and displacement; this study extends it to five landscapes and reads the resulting thresholds against the published ranges. The carbon yield curve approach also offers a route to strategies that optimize carbon and wood supply jointly. 

This study builds on the carbon yield curve method of \cite{hennigar2008novel, neilson2008optimal} and asks a decision question rather than a bookkeeping question: across the range of \ac{HWP} half-lives and displacement factors that the empirical literature supports, which harvest level does an emission-minimizing forest estate model choose - and is the answer a property of the forest landscape or of the parameters? We answer it with an open, integrated framework in which \ac{ws3} optimizes management, libCBM-derived carbon yield curves carry the full ecosystem carbon ledger, and a product-pool model with substitution represents \acp{HWP}, so that carbon is an objective of the optimization itself rather than a post-hoc calculation. We repeat the experiment across five timber supply areas in interior British Columbia to test whether the answer generalizes across landscapes.




Forest management can play a pivotal role in climate change mitigation and sustainable development options, through intentional interventions that impact the forest carbon cycle. In 2007, the \ac{IPCC} recognized all scales of forest management (plot to landscape) as a critical nature-based climate change mitigation strategy.  \citep{Nabuurs2007Forestry}. 

 The \ac{IPCC} further emphasized: “In the long term, a sustainable forest management strategy aimed at maintaining or increasing forest carbon stocks, while producing an annual yield of wood, fiber, or energy from the forest, will generate the largest sustained mitigation benefit” \citep{Nabuurs2007Forestry}. Accordingly, forest carbon management encompasses the actions, plans, and strategies implemented to systematically maintain or increase carbon stocks or sequestration across the entire forest sector, accounting for both forest ecosystems and \acp{HWP}.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}
\subsection{Overview}
\label{sec: overview} 
This study aims to develop a forest carbon management modeling framework to identify forest management strategies that optimize the climate change mitigation potential of forest sectors. To assess the climate impacts of various forest management strategies, the framework integrates the \ac{ws3} to simulate forest growth and optimize forest management activities \citep{Paradis_ws3_a_Wood_2025}, and libCBM \citep{libcbmpy} to estimate long-term carbon stocks and flows within forest ecosystems. \ac{ws3} is an open-source forest estate modeling tool implemented in Python, developed to support \ac{SFM} planning and forest resource analysis. For carbon accounting, the study utilizes libCBM, an open-source implementation of the \ac{CBM-CFS3} \citep{KURZ2009480}, to estimate carbon stocks and their temporal changes within forest ecosystems. To simulate the carbon dynamics of \acp{HWP}, this study integrates the modeling of product pool decomposition with the assessment of substitution effects associated with \acp{HWP}. This study presents and integrative modeling framework to optimize forest management decisions under maximizing carbon stocks and minimizing net carbon emissions.  

Figure \ref{framework} provides an overview of the modeling process used in this study, which is divided into three main stages: data preparation, model execution, and modeling output comparison. The data preparation stage includes an \ac{AOI}, forest inventory data preparation, development of both timber yield and carbon yield curves and compiling \ac{HWP} data. The second stage includes the building of the modeling framework and integration of \ac{ws3} and the \ac{WPCM} to simulate various forest management scenarios. The model objective is twofold: (1) to maximize total carbon storage across both forest ecosystems and harvested wood products, and (2) to minimize net carbon emissions from the entire forest system, accounting for the climate mitigation benefits associated with product substitution. Finally, in the third stage, the optimized outputs generated by the modeling framework are compared against baseline results obtained from libCBM, in order to evaluate the consistency and effectiveness of the proposed approach for modeling carbon within a linear optimization forest supply timber analysis framework.

\subsection{Data Preparation}
\label{sec:case-study}
\subsubsection{Area of Interest}
 The study area is the region analyzed by \cite{boisvenue2022managing}: five timber supply areas (\acp{TSA}) spanning the Interior of \ac{BC}, Canada - \ac{TSA} 08, 16, 24 (Prince George), 40 and 41 (Figure \ref{fig: TSA24_AOI}). The five units range from the wetter northern interior to the drier southern interior, and represent the major forest types and management regimes of the \ac{BC} Interior; \ac{TSA} 24 alone contains $\approx$ 15\% of the provincial \ac{THLB} \citep{princegeorge2017}. The parameter sensitivity is repeated in every unit so that it is a cross-landscape test rather than a single-case study.

\subsubsection{Forest Inventory Data}
The relevant forest inventory data was procured for all five units. This forest inventory data was collected, processed and prepared in a previous study by \citep{boisvenue2022managing}.  The forest inventory includes stand-level attributes, such as stand area, age, leading species composition, site index, \ac{BEC} and delineation in to the \ac{THLB} and \ac{NTHLB}. Leading species, site index and \ac{BEC} were used to stratify the land base in to \acp{AU}. The \ac{AU} classification was the base unit used to determine both timber and carbon yield curves. Every \ac{AU} in the five units was assigned a timber type, ensuring that every \ac{AU} has both a timber and carbon curve. The forest inventory preparation also included descriptive statistics about the characteristics of the \ac{AOI}. 

\subsubsection{Yield Curves}
Two sets of yield curves were needed for this study: timber yield curves and carbon yield curves. The timber yield curves were previously created in the \cite{boisvenue2022managing} study. The yield curves used were divided into two groups. \Ac{VDYP} model generated the timber yield curves for previously unmanaged stands and \ac{TIPSY} program for the generation of timber yield curves for managed stands. Managed vs unmanaged stands were separated based on previous regeneration practices. If the stand had been previously harvested and planted it was considered managed and if the stand was not planted and natural regeneration was allowed to occur the stand was considered unmanaged. Timber yield curves were projected for a 350-year planning horizon. All \acp{AU} were assigned a starting yield curve and a transition curve that gets applied after harvest occurred. 

Carbon yield curves were created following \citep{neilson2008optimal}, integrating carbon into the \ac{SFMM} alongside the timber yield curves. Forest carbon was modelled with libCBM, the open-source implementation of \ac{CBM-CFS3} \citep{KURZ2009480}, across 21 carbon pools (10 biomass and 11 \ac{DOM}) and 52 carbon fluxes. For each analysis unit, a one-hectare stand was simulated on its natural trajectory to the model's typical harvest age (120 years; area-weighted median harvested ages run 110-125 years across the scenario sets), at which point a clearcut harvest event transferred the carbon pools according to CBM's disturbance matrix (merchantable carbon removed; slash and dead roots transferred to the \ac{DOM} pools), and the stand regrew on its managed yield curve. Repeating the harvest at the rotation age until the \ac{DOM} pools reached their cycle equilibrium, and extracting the post-harvest trajectory, yielded the managed carbon yield curves; the natural curves are the no-management trajectories. The projections were aggregated into biomass, merchantable, \ac{DOM} and ecosystem stock curves and one net emission flux curve. This structure allows for carbon to be integrated in the \ac{SFMM} similar to the integration of timber yield curves. Aggregating the carbon curves and fluxes allowed for computational efficiency and sufficient carbon yield accuracy for simulation and optimization. The curves are registered in \ac{ws3} with its default piecewise-linear simplification (relative tolerance $10^{-2}$); the same registered curves are used for both the optimization and the emission reporting, so the carbon accounting remains internally consistent. Additionally, this method leaves space for more detailed or additional carbon pools and fluxes to be added in later studies. 

Dead organic matter initialization in CBM is known to produce upward-biased initial pools: the initialization procedure applies stand-replacing disturbances at regular intervals equal to the fire return interval rather than the stochastic disturbance regime, which over-accumulates carbon relative to the landscape's carbon-holding capacity \citep{boisvenue2022managing}, and initialization choices are a documented source of variation and systematic error in carbon models \citep{metsaranta2017, oberpriller2021}. Because a post-harvest reset to the initialized state would inherit this bias, the managed curves in this study are generated by simulating the harvest itself, so that post-harvest carbon states are conservation-consistent with the harvested stand's pre-harvest state; the residual difference between first-rotation and steady-state harvest histories remains bounded across the five units (net phantom carbon - the harvested-area-weighted residual - between $-0.2$\% and $+2.3$\% of initial ecosystem carbon; record: \texttt{results/cross-tsa/reset\_residuals\_tsa*.csv}). 

\subsubsection{HWP Data}
This study assumed an 85\% utilization amount of harvested wood \citep{smyth2020climate}. The utilized fiber was divided into two equal categories of \acp{HWP}: paper and solid wood products \citep{ipcc2019refinement}. This division of \acp{HWP} was determined based on historical data on log availability and utilization in \ac{BC} \cite{BC_Mill_List_2020}. The carbon content of the harvested wood is taken directly from the libCBM merchantable-carbon yield curves rather than from a separate volume--density conversion, so that the product-pool inflows are consistent with the ecosystem carbon ledger. 
\Ac{LCA} data required to parameterize the \ac{WPCM}, including \ac{HWP} half-lives and displacement factors, were obtained from \cite{smyth2020climate}. The reference parameterization uses a 2-year half-life for paper and a 30-year half-life for solid wood products \citep{eggleston2006ipcc}, with a 2.2 tC/tC displacement factor for solid wood and zero for paper \citep{smyth2020climate}; these values were also used to derive the annual decay constants for first-order decay \citep{eggleston2006ipcc}. Reported half-lives span roughly 1-90 years across commodity classes \citep{ipcc2019refinement} and reported displacement factors about $-0.7$ to 5.1 tC/tC at the 95\% level, centred near 1.2 \citep{leskinen2018}, and forest-sector conclusions are known to depend on these inputs. The reference values are therefore treated as one point in a full parameter sweep rather than as constants (see the sensitivity design below).

\subsubsection{The Sustainable Forest Management Model}
\label{sec: modeling}
This study employed \ac{ws3} which integrates the forest inventory data, timber and carbon yield curves, management actions, transitions and scenario parameters to construct a \ac{SFMM} for simulation and optimization. The optimization model applied in this study to achieve the objective functions and satisfy the constraints across different scenarios was adapted from the work of \citep{paradis2013risk}. 

The mathematical formulation of the proposed model is presented as follows:
\begin{align}
\text{max} & \quad \sum_{i \in Z} \sum_{j \in J_i} c_{ij} x_{ij} \label{eq:1} \\
\text{s.t.} & \quad \sum_{j \in J_i} x_{ij} = 1, \quad \forall i \in Z \label{eq:2} \\
& \quad (1 - \epsilon_p) y_{p1} \leq \sum_{i \in Z} \sum_{j \in J_i} \mu_{ijpt} x_{ij} \leq (1 + \epsilon_p) y_{p1}, \quad \forall p \in O', t \in T \label{eq:3} \\
& \quad v_{ot}^- \leq \sum_{i \in Z} \sum_{j \in J_i} \mu_{ijot} x_{ij} \leq v_{ot}^+, \quad \forall o \in O, t \in T \label{eq:4} \\
& \quad 0 \leq x_{ij} \leq 1, \quad \forall i \in Z, j \in J_i \label{eq:5}
\end{align}
where:
\begin{align*}
Z &:= \text{set of spatial zones} \\
J_i &:= \text{set of available prescriptions for zone } i \in Z \\
O &:= \text{set of forest outputs} \\
O' &\subseteq O := \text{set of targeted forest outputs} \\
T &:= \text{set of periods in the planning horizon} \\
\epsilon_p &:= \text{admissible level of variation on yield of targeted output } p \in O' \\
\mu_{ijot} &:= \text{quantity of output } o \in O \text{ produced in period } t \in T \notag \\
           &\quad \text{by prescription } j \in J_i \text{ in zone } i \in Z \\
v_{ot}^- &:= \text{lower bound on yield of output } o \in O \text{ in period } t \in T \\
v_{ot}^+ &:= \text{upper bound on yield of output } o \in O \text{ in period } t \in T \\
c_{ij} &:= \text{objective function contribution of prescription } j \in J_i \text{ in zone } i \in Z \\
x_{ij} &:= \text{proportion of zone } i \in Z \text{ on which prescription } j \in J_i \text{ is applied} \\
y_{p1} &:= \sum_{i \in Z} \sum_{j \in J_i} \mu_{ijp1} x_{ij}, \notag \\
       &\quad \text{i.e. first-period harvest volume for targeted output } p \in O'
\end{align*}
The objective function (\ref{eq:1}) maximizes the desired result based on the applied prescriptions. For this study a no harvest option (i.e. do nothing) was considered a valid prescription  in addition to more standard prescriptions, including harvesting.  Constraint sets specify that the total proportion of prescriptions applied to each zone must be equal to one  (\ref{eq:2}) . Then an even-flow constraint set was created that ensured consistency in target outputs (\ref{eq:3}).  Constraint sets  enforce upper and lower bounds on the yields in each period (\ref{eq:4}). The domain of the decision variables is defined by constraint sets (\ref{eq:5}).

\subsubsection{The Wood Product Carbon Model}
\label{sec: wood model}
The \ac{WPCM} developed for this study serves two primary functions: (1) tracking the carbon stocks and the emissions associated with \acp{HWP} derived from harvested timber over the simulation horizon, and (2) estimating the substitution effects of  \acp{HWP}. The constraints are same as those defined in the \ac{SFMM}. Additionally, the constraints sets are identical to those defined in the \ac{SFMM}; however, the objective function is reformulated to either maximize carbon stocks or minimize net emissions.

\paragraph{Maximize the Total Forest System Carbon Stock}
This objective is to maximize the total carbon stock of the entire forest system (Eq. \ref{eq:6}), where the total carbon stock is defined as the sum of the carbon stored in the forest ecosystem and in \acp{HWP} (Eq. \ref{eq:7}). 

\begin{align}
\max \sum_{t \in T} C_t^{\text{sys}}
\label{eq:6}
\end{align}

\begin{equation}
C_t^{\text{sys}}= C_t^{\text{eco}}+ C_t^{\text{hwp}}
\label{eq:7}
\end{equation}

where,
\begin{align*}
C^{\text{sys}}_t &:= \text{Carbon stock of the entire forest system in period } t \in T\\
C^{\text{eco}}_t &:= \text{Carbon stock of the forest ecosystem in period } t \in T\\
C^{\text{hwp}}_t &:= \text{Carbon stock of all {HWP} in period } t \in T
\end{align*}

Carbon stock of all \acp{HWP}, $C^{\text{hwp}}_t$, is defined through Eq.~\ref{eq:8} to Eq.~\ref{eq:11}.


\begin{equation}
C^{\text{hwp}}_t = \sum_{p \in P}C_{\text{p,t}}
\label{eq:8}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
C_{\text{p,t}} = a_{\text{p}}C_t^{\text{wood}}
\label{eq:9}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
C_t^{\text{wood}} = u \, M_t
\label{eq:10}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\sum_{p \in P}a_{\text{p}} = 1
\label{eq:11}
\end{equation}

where,

\begin{align*}
    P &:= \text{Set of products} \\
    M_t &:= \text{Merchantable carbon of the stands harvested in period $t \in T$ (tC)} \\
    u &:= \text{Utilization rate of harvested wood (0.85)} \\
    C^{\text{wood}} &:= \text{Carbon stock of harvested wood entering the product pools (tC)} \\
    C_{\text{p,t}} &:= \text{Carbon stock of product $p \in P$ in time period $t \in T$ (tC)} \\
    a_{\text{p}} &:= \text{Allocation factor for product $p \in P$}
\end{align*}

Once a \ac{HWP} enters its life cycle, the \ac{IPCC} first-order decay model \citep{eggleston2006ipcc} is applied to calculate changes in the \ac{HWP} carbon stocks over time  (Eq. \ref{eq:12}). The half-life of each product is then used to determine the corresponding annual decay constant (Eq. \ref{eq:13}), and the ten-year survival factor, $e^{-10 k_{\text{p}}} = 2^{-10/hl_{\text{p}}}$, is applied directly to each product pool.

\begin{equation}
C_{\text{p,t+1}} = C_{\text{p, t}} \, e^{-10 k_{\text{p}}}
\label{eq:12}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
k_{\text{p}} = \frac{\ln(2)}{hl_{\text{p}}}
\label{eq:13}
\end{equation}

where,
\begin{align*}
    k_{\text{p}} &:= \text{Annual decay constant of first-order decay for product $p \in P$} \\
    hl_{\text{p}} &:= \text{Half-life for product $p \in P$ in years} \end{align*}


\paragraph{Minimize the Net Forest Carbon Emission}
The objective function (Eq. \ref{eq:14}) minimizes the net carbon emissions from the forest sector, defined as the sum of carbon emissions from the forest ecosystem and \acp{HWP}, minus the substitution effect of \acp{HWP} (Eq. \ref{eq:15}). 
The accounting is implemented as a stock-difference formulation, $E_{sys}(t) = [E(t) - E(t+1)] + [H(t) - H(t+1)] - S_t^{\text{hwp}}$, where $E(t)$ and $H(t)$ are the ecosystem and harvested wood product carbon stocks at period $t$, so that summing over the planning horizon telescopes exactly to the total system carbon change minus the substitution effect; the objective is therefore carbon-conservative by construction. The harvested wood product ledger tracks product pools only (harvest residue and field losses are inside the ecosystem stock change), with the utilized fibre split equally between paper and solid wood products as described above. 

\begin{align}
\min \sum_{t \in T} E_t^{\text{sys}}
\label{eq:14}
\end{align}

\begin{equation}
E_t^{\text{sys}} = \frac{44}{12}(E_t^{\text{eco}}+ E_t^{\text{hwp}} - S_t^{\text{hwp}})
\label{eq:15}
\end{equation}



where,
\begin{itemize}
    \item $E_t^{\text{sys}}$ := $ \mathrm{CO}_2 $ emission of the whole forest system in the period $t \in T$.
    \item $E_t^{\text{eco}}$ := $ \mathrm{CO}_2 $ emission of the forest ecosystem in the period $t \in T$.
    \item $E_t^{\text{hwp}}$ := $ \mathrm{CO}_2 $ emission of all \acp{HWP} in the period $t \in T$.
    \item $S_t^{\text{hwp}}$ := Substitution effect of all \acp{HWP} in the period $t \in T$.
\end{itemize}

In this study, \acp{HWP} carbon emissions, $E_t^{\text{hwp}}$, are calculated from annual changes in carbon stocks within each \acp{HWP} pool. The emitted carbon is subsequently converted to \ac{CO$_2$} as shown in Eq. (\ref{eq:16}).
\begin{equation}
E_{t+1}^{\text{hwp}} = \frac{44}{12}(C_{t}^{\text{hwp}} - C_{t+1}^{\text{hwp}})
\label{eq:16}
\end{equation}
Here a decrease in the product-pool carbon stock appears as a positive emission.
The substitution effect of \acp{HWP}, $S_t^{\text{hwp}}$, is calculated from the carbon inflow into each product pool using their displacement factors (Eqs. \ref{eq:17} and \ref{eq:18}); displacement applies to the solid-wood inflow, while the paper displacement factor is zero. The solid-wood inflow is the utilized merchantable carbon - 0.85 utilization times the 50/50 paper--solid allocation - so displacement factors are applied per tonne of carbon in solid-wood products; one unit of displacement factor thus provides 0.425 tC of effective credit per tC of harvested merchantable carbon (record: \texttt{results/cross-tsa/df\_convention\_mapping.csv}). 
\begin{equation}
S_t^{\text{hwp}} = \sum_{p \in P}S_{\text{p,t}}
\label{eq:17}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
S_{\text{p,t}} = a_{\text{p}}C_t^{\text{wood}}df_{\text{p}}
\label{eq:18}
\end{equation}
where, 
\begin{align*}
    S_{\text{p,t}} &:= \text{Substitution effect of the period-}t\text{ inflow to product } p \in P \\
    df_{\text{p}} &:= \text{Displacement factor for product $p \in P$ } \end{align*}

\subsection{Model Execution}
\label{sec: Model Execution}
For the simulations and optimizations in this study, a 200-year time horizon (beginning in 2020) was adopted and divided into 20 periods of 10 years each. In each optimization problem, the even-flow constraints were applied to both harvest volume and harvest area, restricting their variation between consecutive periods to less than 5\%. Harvest volume refers to the utilized volume (merchantable volume multiplied by the 85\% utilization rate) throughout this study.

\subsubsection{Carbon Stocks}
Unlike conventional carbon accounting approaches, this study approximates forest carbon stocks and emissions and integrates them with the optimization model. Thus, it is necessary to evaluate and compare the model outputs directly to the forest carbon budget model results. This was done through a landscape-level no-harvest comparison, allowing for direct comparison of the carbon outputs from the new proposed model to the reference model libCBM.

\subsubsection{Half-life and Displacement Sensitivity Analysis}
To assess how the optimal harvest level responds to \acp{HWP} parameters, each management unit is re-optimized on a full grid of half-lives and displacement factors: solid-wood half-lives from 1 to 200 years (200 integer values) crossed with displacement factors from 0 to 2.4 tC/tC in 0.1 steps (25 values) - 5,000 emission-minimizing scenarios per unit and 25,000 in total. The reference parameterization (30-year solid-wood half-life, 2.2 tC/tC) is one of the 5,000 cells. Paper half-life (2 years) and the 50/50 paper--solid allocation are held at their reference values, and substitution applies to the solid-wood inflow only (Eqs. \ref{eq:17} and \ref{eq:18}). Each scenario is a landscape-level linear program that maximizes negative net system emissions under 5\% even-flow constraints on harvested area and volume, solved with HiGHS; harvest levels are reported as fractions of each unit's base scenario (the volume-maximizing even-flow solution), so that units of different size are directly comparable.

A cohort-exact harvested-landscape cross-validation against libCBM remains future work: the ws3-to-libCBM replay for managed stands is implemented with its conventions pinned, but the cohort-exact comparison still carries an unresolved residual, so we do not report a harvested-landscape stock or flux comparison here. \subsection{Minimize Net Forest Carbon Emissions}

\acp{HWP} and displacement enter the optimization through the product-pool model and the substitution credit, and the emission-minimizing solution responds strongly to both. Figure \ref{fig: 3d_graph} maps the optimal utilized harvest volume over the full parameter plane for the five management units; the TSA 24 row illustrates the common structure. For displacement factors below roughly 0.5 tC/tC the optimal solution harvests little at any half-life (in TSA 24, under 8\% of the base scenario). As the displacement factor rises the optimal harvest level climbs steeply and approaches the volume-maximizing solution, reaching $1.07\times10^9$ m$^3$ (76\% of the base scenario) at a 200-year half-life and a displacement factor of 2.4 tC/tC. Half-life moves the threshold rather than determining it: at a 100-year half-life the mid-point of the transition - the displacement factor at which the optimal harvest crosses half of its observed response range - is 1.61 tC/tC, and it stays between 1.47 and 1.79 as the half-life spans 1-200 years.

The same qualitative flip appears in all five units, but its location is a property of the landscape as much as of the parameters (Figure \ref{fig: cross_tsa}, Table \ref{tab:units}). Mid-point thresholds at a 100-year half-life range from 0.95 tC/tC (TSA 08) to 1.61 tC/tC (TSA 24), and the onset of the transition (10\% of each unit's response range) varies more than five-fold at the same half-life, from 0.17 to 0.87 tC/tC. The spread of mid-point thresholds across units (0.66 tC/tC at a 100-year half-life) is comparable to the shift produced by moving the half-life across its full 1-200-year range within a unit (0.24-1.08 tC/tC depending on the unit). The forms of the flip also differ: in TSA 16 the emission-minimizing solution is complete cessation - zero harvest - for displacement factors up to 0.4 tC/tC at every half-life and for most half-lives at 0.5 tC/tC (1,446 of 5,000 scenarios), the strongest form of the flip in the set, while in TSA 08 no scenario fully ceases harvesting. The units span a five-fold range in harvested area and more than a four-fold range in initial ecosystem carbon, yet the thresholds do not order with unit size or starting carbon (|Spearman $\rho$| $\le$ 0.6; record: \texttt{results/cross-tsa/threshold\_attribute\_correlations.csv}). Their strongest correlate is age structure: the mid-point threshold rises monotonically with the share of the landbase younger than 50 years, from 7\% in TSA 08 (the lowest threshold) to 41\% in TSA 24 (the highest; $\rho = 1.0$ across the five units at a 100-year half-life and 0.9 for the median threshold across half-lives). Carbon density ranks in the opposite direction ($\rho = -0.8$, $p = 0.10$), and with five units age structure, productivity and site carbon cannot be separated - the mechanism is left to a wider instance set. Because the prescription set of this model is clearcut harvesting versus no harvest - the carbon curves are compiled on a fixed 120-year regeneration cycle while the optimizer selects from operable stands - these results characterize the climate-optimal harvest level, not silvicultural systems.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
\subsection{Model Performance}
When the \ac{SFMM} was set to maximize the harvested timber volume (Figure \ref{fig: area_vol_stock}), the even-flow constraints act as imposed limits on the harvest area ($ha$). This causes limited variation and stability in the harvested area and harvested timber volume over the 200-year planning horizon. 

Although our model and libcbm use similar inputs such as forest inventory, yield curves, and treatment transitions to estimate forest carbon dynamics, they differ fundamentally in the forest carbon modeling mechanism, which can potentially lead to divergent outputs. libcbm includes a built-in carbon accounting framework that explicitly simulates fluxes between carbon pools and tracks carbon stocks using deterministic logic parametrized by either built-in default or user-override flux logic parameters \citep{libcbmpy}. In contrast, our model lacks an internal carbon model and instead relies on carbon stock and flux yield curves generated from libcbm as an approximation.



The comparisons between our model and libcbm support the corrected curve layer. In the no-harvest scenario the forest ecosystem functions as a persistent carbon sink in both implementations, and the stock trajectories agree closely in all five units - period-mean absolute differences of 0.4-3.1\% (ecosystem), 0.2-0.5\% (biomass) and 0.6-5.4\% (\ac{DOM}), within 1\% on every layer in four of the five units - and by the final period biomass agrees to within 1\% in every unit, ecosystem and \ac{DOM} within 1\% in four of five units (3.1\% and 4.0\% in TSA 08). This close alignment confirms that the carbon yield curve method represents unmanaged landscape carbon dynamics at the accuracy required by the optimization; without harvesting, the two accounting implementations are effectively interchangeable for decision purposes.

Under active harvest, no corrected cross-implementation comparison is reported: the cohort-exact ws3-to-libCBM replay for managed stands still carries an unresolved residual, so a harvested-landscape validation is listed as future work rather than approximated with pre-correction numbers. For optimization, the decisive safeguards are structural rather than comparative: the reported emission ledger telescopes exactly, successive stock differences cancel, and the optimization objective and the reported system emission are computed from the same accounting, an identity verified to solver precision in the regression suite (an independent audit additionally re-solved four committed grid scenarios end to end with exact agreement).

From a climate change mitigation perspective, two key forest management strategies are widely discussed. One considers forests as carbon reservoirs, where conservation policies aim to preserve and increase forest carbon stocks. The other recommends intensified forest management, producing \acp{HWP} whose use can lead to displacement effects that potentially reduce ecosystem emissions. Within the scientific community, however, there is still no consensus on which approach provides the best climate benefit. A group of studies (e.g. \citep{gustavsson2017climate}, \citep{heinonen2017scenario}) concluded that increasing harvesting level can lead to more climate benefits while other researchers (e.g. \cite{leturcq2020ghg}, \cite{skytt2021climate}, \cite{soimakallio2022closing}) found increased harvesting counterproductive for climate change mitigation, including a recent analysis of north temperate rainforests in British Columbia projecting lower emissions by 2050 under reduced logging intensity and residue burning \citep{dymond2025reducing}. The five-landscape experiment sharpens this debate. The displacement factors at which the optimal harvest flips lie between roughly 0.5 and 1.8 tC/tC depending on the unit and half-life - inside the range of published substitution estimates (Figure \ref{fig: overlay}), where a 51-study compilation \citep{leskinen2018} reports a mean near 1.2 with a 95\% range exceeding 5 - and the flip's location moves as much with the management unit as with the half-life. Read directly against the published ranges, the decision consequence is quantifiable: across the 95\% range of the 51-study compilation the optimal harvest level for a single unit spans 1-76\% of the volume-maximizing base (TSA 24 over the tested half-life span), and a single Canadian life-cycle source \citep{smyth2020climate} implies opposite ends of that spectrum depending on the end-use assumption - 3-7\% of the base under the general end-use basket (0.45-0.54 tC/tC) versus 36-71\% under the building-only high case (2.1-2.2 tC/tC), with TSA 16 at complete cessation under the former and up to half the base under the latter (record: \texttt{results/cross-tsa/decision\_under\_evidence.csv}). The climate verdict on a given harvest level is therefore not identifiable from current \ac{HWP} evidence: it is set by product lifetime and substitution, which current evidence bounds only loosely. Rather than assuming those values and deriving a harvest target from them, the policy question becomes whether and how they can be pinned down. Unlike soil carbon or site productivity, product lifetime and substitution are contractible attributes of the downstream supply chain: building codes, procurement standards, product declarations and monitoring can fix them over the horizons over which the climate benefit is claimed. We therefore suggest that the debate over whether harvesting is good or bad for the climate be reframed as a question of which product lifetimes and substitution performance are actually locked in, and that forest-climate policies state those assumptions explicitly, because the optimal harvest level lies on the other side of them.

The breadth of the transition matters in its own right. Because the optimal harvest level can swing from its unit's low-displacement harvest level toward the volume-maximizing level across a wide displacement-factor band that occupies much of the tested parameter range - 10-90\% widths of the response range give a median of 1.8 tC/tC at a 100-year half-life on the 0-2.4 tC/tC tested axis - the recommended strategy is sensitive to where inside that band the true parameters fall: modest errors or changes in the assumed values - or in trade conditions, tariffs and fibre demand that alter effective substitution and product life - can shift it substantially. The five-landscape spread shows that even with accurate parameters the answer remains unit-specific, but parameter uncertainty dominates the practical risk.



\subsection{Limitations}
The emission-minimizing objective telescopes: the per-period terms collapse to terminal system stock plus cumulative substitution, so the optimal harvest level is explicitly a property of the chosen horizon (200 years here, held fixed across all scenarios and units). The prescription set distinguishes only clearcut harvesting (operable stands aged 80-400 years) from no harvest; harvest rotation age, partial harvesting and reservation strategies are outside the experiment, and the carbon curves are compiled on a fixed 120-year regeneration cycle, and the width of the even-flow band shapes the feasible set (5\% here; six one-at-a-time variants re-run on the campaign model at three reference cells (at a 100-year half-life; displacement factors 0.5, 1.0 and 2.0 tC/tC) leave the low-displacement suppression of harvest intact in every variant - 2.9-10.3 times less volume at 0.5 than at 2.0 tC/tC - while the optimal harvested volume varies 3.6-14-fold across variants at a given cell and net system emissions stay within $-16\%$ to $+18\%$ of the baseline, except under an all-solid-wood allocation ($+80\%$ at 2.0 tC/tC); record: \texttt{results/femic-tsa24/robustness/}); rotation age is fixed by the curve compilation). Solutions are linear-program vertices, so each cell reports one optimal schedule rather than a full description of the solution space. The carbon curves carry \ac{ws3}'s default piecewise-linear simplification (relative tolerance $10^{-2}$), and the rotation-cycle spin-up leaves a first-rotation to steady-state residual bounded at 2.3\% of initial ecosystem carbon in the worst of the five units (legacy TSA 24 calibration: 1.6\%; record: \texttt{results/cross-tsa/reset\_residuals\_tsa*.csv}). A corrected harvested-landscape cross-validation against libCBM is not yet available (the cohort-exact replay for managed stands carries an unresolved residual), as discussed above; this remains the main validation gap at the landscape level, partially offset by the stand-level harvest-cycle check reported above. Finally, substitution is applied to solid-wood inflows only, under a 50/50 paper-solid allocation with no paper substitution; displacement factors are interpreted per tonne of carbon in solid-wood products (production-stage, forest carbon excluded, following \citealp{leskinen2018}), so the reported mid-point thresholds correspond to effective credits of 0.40-0.68 tC per tonne of harvested merchantable carbon.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This study developed an open, integrated forest carbon management framework that couples \ac{ws3} estate optimization with libCBM-derived carbon yield curves and a harvested wood product model, and applied it across five timber supply areas in interior British Columbia, Canada - 25,000 emission-minimizing harvest schedules across half-lives spanning the reported commodity range and displacement factors bracketing the central substitution estimates.

The framework's correction over prior yield-curve integrations is structural: harvested stands are simulated through the clearcut itself, so post-harvest carbon states are conservation-consistent with the pre-harvest stand; the reported emission ledger telescopes exactly; and the optimization objective and the reported emissions are computed from the same accounting, an identity verified to solver precision. The curve layer reproduces libCBM's no-harvest landscape stocks within a few percent in every unit.

The central result is that the climate-optimal harvest level is not identifiable from current \ac{HWP} evidence. In every unit the emission-minimizing harvest level flips from its unit's low-displacement harvest level toward the volume-maximizing level across a displacement-factor band whose mid-points at a 100-year half-life span 0.95-1.61 tC/tC - inside the empirically reported range of substitution estimates. The flip's location shifts as much with the management unit as with the half-life, one unit optimally stops harvesting altogether below about 0.5 tC/tC, and half-life moves the threshold far less than displacement moves the outcome. Because the parameters that decide the answer are contractible attributes of the product supply chain, we argue the harvest-climate debate should move from proving that harvesting is good or bad toward specifying and monitoring the product lifetimes and substitution on which its climate value depends - and that policy analyses state those assumptions explicitly.
