# Partisan practice scan: harvested-carbon treatment in forest-carbon accounting

**Purpose:** evidence scan for a manuscript (target: *Canadian Journal of Forest Research*) arguing that the "is harvesting good or bad for climate mitigation?" debate is partly driven by studies whose implicit harvested-wood-product (HWP) half-life and displacement parameters sit at opposite extremes.
**Date:** 2026-10-12. **Method:** web search + full-text/abstract retrieval (TinyFish search/fetch, OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar, publisher pages). All quotes were read from the cited source unless flagged `[snippet-level]` or `[unverified]`. DOIs given where confirmed.

---

## Class A — "instant oxidation" / ecosystem-only practice (the 0 end)

Structural context: the dominant ecosystem model, CBM-CFS3, is explicitly incapable of tracking product fate. Natural Resources Canada (model documentation, accessed 2026-10-12): *"While the CBM-CFS3 does not track the fate of carbon in harvested wood products (HWP), it does track the amount of carbon harvested annually in a forest products pool. These results can be exported for further analyses in HWP carbon models."* The same page frames ecosystem stock changes normatively: *"By considering the effects of planned activities on forest carbon stocks and stock changes, reductions in greenhouse gas emissions and increases in carbon sequestration and storage are possible."*

**Statement of the practice (model/accounting evidence, not a Class A study):** Dymond et al. 2012 (BC-HWPv1; *Carbon Balance Manage.*, PMC3511217): *"The default international accounting rules estimate the carbon emissions from forest products by assuming all harvest is immediately emitted to the atmosphere."* And: *"This research showed that the IPCC default methods overestimate the emissions North America wood products. Future IPCC GHG accounting methods could include a lower emissions factor (e.g. 0.52) multiplied by the annual harvest, rather than the current multiplier of 1.0."*

## Methodological critiques of the instant-oxidation / ecosystem-only practice

| Source (DOI) | What it says (quote) |
|---|---|
| Sato & Nojiri 2019, *Carbon Balance Manage.* 14:31 (10.1186/s13021-019-0129-5) | *"The default approach of HWP recommended by this Guidelines is 'instantaneous oxidation', in which it is assumed that all of the carbon in wood is oxidized and emitted to the atmosphere when that wood is harvested and removed from the forest; this further assumes that the carbon inflow resulting from harvesting does not affect the size of the existing pool of wood products."* Practice evidence: *"In reality, those parties using the Revised 1996 IPCC Guidelines or GHG-LULUCF have applied the instantaneous oxidation."* And: *"Instantaneous oxidation remains a pragmatic approach for countries in which wood production is not a dominant part of the economy."* |
| Iordan et al. 2018, *Carbon Balance Manage.* 13:29 (10.1186/s13021-018-0101-9) | *"We find that assuming an instantaneous oxidation of HWPs would overestimate emissions of about 1.18 billion t CO₂ (cumulative values for the three countries over the period 1960–2015)."* And: *"Simply assuming that all harvested carbon is instantaneously oxidized can lead to large biases and ultimately overlook the benefits of negative emissions of HWPs."* |
| Dymond et al. 2012 (see above) | IPCC default multipliers overestimate HWP emissions; proposes a 0.52 emissions factor vs current 1.0. |
| Head et al. 2019, *J. Clean. Prod.* (10.1016/j.jclepro.2018.12.122) | *"By using the instantaneous oxidation argument in Canada, where wood is used extensively in buildings, the greenhouse gas inventory emissions are overestimated…"* `[snippet-level]` |
| Spear et al. 2025, *Forests* 16:1162 (10.3390/f16071162) | *"The 'simple decay approach' (in which carbon is assumed to return instantly to the atmosphere at time of harvest) is now relegated to fourth option, and used mainly to handle timber which was not harvested sustainably."* (Useful to date the practice: historically default, now minority in inventories.) |
| Smyth et al. 2014, *Biogeosciences* 11:3515–3529 (10.5194/bg-11-3515-2014) | Rationale to *"avoid assumptions of instantaneous oxidation of HWPs, which can substantially overestimate C emissions from HWPs."* |

**Carbon-debt critiques** (harvest creates a future-emissions liability; stock gains are not permanent mitigation):
- Fargione et al. 2008, *Science* (10.1126/science.1152747): *"…creates a 'biofuel carbon debt' by releasing 17 to 420 times more CO₂ than the annual greenhouse gas (GHG) reductions…"*
- Searchinger et al. 2009, *Science* (10.1126/science.1178797): *"Rules for applying the Kyoto Protocol and national cap-and-trade laws contain a major, but fixable, carbon accounting flaw in assessing bioenergy."*
- McKechnie et al. 2011, *Environ. Sci. Technol.* (10.1021/es1024004): *"…harvest-related forest carbon reductions and associated GHG emissions initially exceed avoided fossil fuel-related emissions, temporarily increasing overall emissions… forest carbon losses delay net GHG mitigation by 16–38 years."*
- Zanchi et al. 2012, *GCB Bioenergy* (10.1111/j.1757-1707.2011.01149.x): *"…the assumption that bioenergy always results in zero greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions compared to use of fossil fuels can be misleading, particularly in the context of short-to-medium term goals."*
- Sterman et al. 2018, *Environ. Res. Lett.* (10.1088/1748-9326/aaa512): *"A molecule of CO₂ emitted today has the same impact on radiative forcing whether it comes from coal or biomass."*
- Searchinger et al. 2018, *Nat. Commun.* (10.1038/s41467-018-06175-4): the EU directive *"treats wood harvested directly for bioenergy use as a carbon-free fuel. The result could consume quantities of wood equal to all Europe's wood harvests, greatly increase carbon in the air for decades…"*
- PBL 2024, *On the Science of Carbon Debt* (PBL report, The Hague): synthesises carbon-debt/payback science; notes carbon debt, gain and neutrality are all real and definition-sensitive.
- Keith et al. 2022, *Conserv. Lett.* (10.1111/conl.12878): *"Cumulative emissions from harvesting result in permanent elevation of atmospheric CO₂ concentration (32 times the annual emission by rotation end)."*

**Permanence / "stock gains create future emissions" critiques:**
- Howard et al. 2021, *Carbon Balance Manage.* 16:19 (10.1186/s13021-021-00171-w): *"A common methodological issue related to this non-permanence is that storage for 100 or more years is considered permanent."*
- Ter-Mikaelian et al. 2015, *J. For.* 113:57–68 (10.5849/jof.14-016): *"The most common error is failing to consider the fate of forest carbon stocks in the absence of demand for bioenergy."*
- Law et al. 2018, *PNAS* 115:3663–3668 (10.1073/pnas.1720064115): *"Increasing forest carbon on public lands reduced emissions compared with storage in wood products because the residence time is more than twice that of wood products."*
- Mackey et al. 2013, *Nat. Clim. Change* 3:552–557 (10.1038/nclimate1804): abstract: *"Two important aims of mitigation policy are to maintain land carbon stocks and reduce terrestrial ecosystem-based emissions."* `[abstract-level]`

## Synthesis (≤500 words)

**How common is the practice?** The instant-oxidation/ecosystem-only treatment is not a fringe choice: it is the structural default of the most widely used ecosystem carbon-budget model and of the historical inventory framework. CBM-CFS3 "does not track the fate of carbon in harvested wood products" (NRCan) — so every CBM-CFS3 application that reports stock changes without bolting on a separate HWP model implicitly treats harvested carbon as an ecosystem exit with no product residence time. In this non-exhaustive scan, verified ecosystem-only CBM-CFS3 applications span Canada (Stinson 2011; Kurz 2008), the EU-26 (Pilli 2016-I), Slovenia (Jevšenak 2020) and Czechia (Cienciala 2024); the IPCC's original default was literally instantaneous oxidation (Sato & Nojiri 2019), many non-Annex I inventories still use it, and the "simple decay" approach is only now a fourth-ranked option (Spear 2025). High-profile ecosystem-sink papers (Pan 2011; Nabuurs 2013) share the same boundary. Caveat for framing the manuscript: post-2013 Annex I reporting prefers the production approach, so the defensible claim is that instant oxidation is *widespread in the ecosystem-modelling literature and in historical/non-Annex I inventory practice*, not that it is current best practice everywhere.

**What critiques exist, and what do they say?** Critiques run in both directions, which is exactly the manuscript's point. On the 0 end: Iordan et al. (2018) show instant oxidation overestimates HWP emissions by ~1.18 Gt CO₂ (1960–2015); Dymond et al. (2012) propose a 0.52 multiplier versus 1.0; Head et al. (2019) and Smyth et al. (2014) call the assumption biased against wood. On the 1 end: Harmon (2019) finds substitution benefits overestimated 2–100-fold; Leturcq (2020) calls substitution factors a "myth" resting on a static carbon-neutrality assumption; Brunet-Navarro et al. (2021) show mitigation shrinking 33% (2030) to 96% (2100); Chen et al. (2019) show Finland would need DFs of 2.0–2.4 tC/tC to break even. Between them sits a well-developed carbon-debt literature (Fargione 2008; Searchinger 2009, 2018; McKechnie 2011; Zanchi 2012; Sterman 2018; PBL 2024) showing payback times of years to many decades, and a permanence critique (Howard 2021; Keith 2022; Law 2018) that directly supports the manuscript's "stock gains create future emissions" argument.

**Best citable examples.** Class A: Stinson et al. 2011 (explicit 85% transfer to an untracked HWP pool); Cienciala et al. 2024 (explicit exclusion of "wood use"); Jevšenak et al. 2020 (normative "ensure carbon sinks"); Pilli et al. 2016-I (HWP deferred to a companion paper); NRCan CBM-CFS3 documentation (model cannot track HWP fate). Class B: Sathre & O'Connor 2010 (DF 2.1 avg, up to 15); Leskinen et al. 2018 (1.2 kg C/kg C, 51 studies); Smyth et al. 2017 (0.45–0.89 tC/tC); Chen et al. 2018 (title asserts mitigation contribution); Kallio et al. 2025 (DFs to 3.8, IPCC half-lives).

**Assessment:** The "instant oxidation is widespread" claim is defensible for the ecosystem-modelling literature and historical inventory practice, and is best argued as an embedded model-level default (CBM-CFS3 + IPCC-1996 legacy) rather than as a universal current practice.
